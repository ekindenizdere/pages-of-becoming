To what degree is the form of life I am living today necessary? To what degree did the decisions I made necessitate my current conditions? What will necessarily happen in the future given my present behavior?

In my personal metaphysics, everything is contingent and necessity is a temporary stabilization of a given configuration (con + figurare, meaning forming together) for a certain amount of time. Configurations are contingent; they form and de-form, and they never re-form in the same way. "Upon those who step into the same rivers, different and again different waters flow" (Heraclitus, DK 22B49a). The same river is named, but the waters are never the same; the same self is named, but the configuration from which each action emerges never repeats itself.

If I look at a single decision, I see that it does not stand alone but comes as a configuration. I imagine a network representation, where nodes are connected by edges. The nodes represent individual events, and the edges give form to the configuration. My decision is one such node, connected to other nodes: my past habits, my mood, my body, the people around me, the pressures I feel, and the possibilities I imagine it might lead to.

When I imagine the future, I do not imagine it as an isolated point or a single outcome. I imagine a configuration, a forming together of factors from the past, present, and future, that might stabilize. For example, if I wake up early, read, write, and continue my work, I begin to see a future in which I become a certain kind of person. I am what I repeatedly do. If I stop doing these things, another pattern appears. If I keep doing something else, a different pattern will stabilize. The more a pattern repeats, the more it starts to feel, and even become, necessary.

Now, what exactly is necessary? The word comes from Latin necessitas, from ne- (not) and cedere (to yield). Necessity, then, is the stabilization of contingency. It is what does not yield to me in the moment, even though it will eventually destabilize. It is that which does not accept your authority, that which is indifferent to your will, that which negates your will. Yet you are always part of the configuration that forms or de-forms, the temporary stabilization, necessity includes you in its definition, while at the same time negating you. Importantly, it is dynamic by virtue of being a temporary stability in a changing field rather than a fixed law.

I believe it is fruitful to distinguish between (1) retrospective necessity, (2) prospective necessity, and (3) immanent necessity.

(1) Retrospective necessity, I define as everything that has happened in the past. It does not matter whether an event was necessary at the time it occurred for it to be necessary in hindsight. It is seen as necessary today simply because it belongs to the past and we cannot change the past. Most events are contingent at the moment they occur, but retrospectively necessary once they have happened, that is, fixed and unyielding to our authority after they take place. What happened happened because of a configuration involving our actions and decisions, but the moment we decide or act, we lose most of our power over that configuration because some of its components become rigid. We can continue to add to the broader configuration and eventually destabilize or deform it, forming a new one, but that specific configuration will forever remain what it has been.  When talking about retrospective necessity, then, I am using “necessary” in the sense of irreversible or unalterable, not in the modal sense of “could not have been otherwise.” More often than not, it could have been otherwise, but it can no longer be otherwise.

What is tricky here is that often the time span in which we make a decision is frighteningly short, and often we decide instinctively rather than through long deliberation. Life would be very difficult if each decision required prolonged reflection, and it is indeed challenging for those who either over-deliberate while making decisions or under-deliberate, that is, are unaware that they are deciding. In any case, what matters here is not whether the decision was "good". We make so many decisions, and each could have created alternative realities for us, could have made us a different person. But we lose authority over what we have done the moment we have done it. The moment a deed is committed, it escapes the domain of my will. The moment we commit a deed, that deed becomes a retrospective necessity. Doubt in matters of retrospective necessity is an autoimmune disease: it is us attacking ourselves by thinking, “Why did I do this? Could I have done it differently? Why did it have to be this way?” These questions arise because, at the moment of action, things were contingent. But once the action has taken place, the alternatives no longer exist. All the possibilities have collapsed into actuality.

(2) Prospective necessity is when we have assumptions about what our actions will lead to. It is a weaker type of necessity than the retrospective, because when we make judgments about future events, we have no choice but to consider probabilities. Prospective necessity is necessarily probabilistic, related to expectations and plans. For example: If I do X, I will end up with Y. I want to end up with Y, so I should do X. This seems like logical reasoning, but it actually does not have much force in the dynamic, participatory nature of human existence because the connection between X and Y is never strictly necessary. Let's take a more concrete example:  If I study, I will pass the exam. I want to pass the exam, so I should study. This would read, in Aristotelian form: All who study pass; I study; therefore, I must pass. In Stoic form: If studying leads to passing, I study; therefore, I must pass. But in lived human experience, the major premise is never necessary. We can never be sure that we will pass the exam if we study. We can be reasonably certain that the connection between studying and passing is strong and rational to rely on, but it is not necessary in the same sense that the past events are necessary in hindsight. The past is closed, we do not participate in its world anymore, but the future is as wide open as it could be; it is at every moment shaped by what we currently think or do. The tricky part here is, of course, being able to separate the present from the future, the immanent necessity from prospective necessity.

(3) Immanent necessity is the necessity of the present configuration. It is what must hold for the system to remain viable. The present necessity cannot exist in an isolated fashion. It should be thought of as the bridge between what was necessary and what will be necessary, it is our present actions that either sustain or break the configuration but it is this configuration (through its constraints) that allows for present necessity in the first place. The present carries traces of the past and is already oriented toward the future. In short, immanent necessity is where retrospective necessity and prospective necessity meet. The past constrains it (and is contained by it), the future attracts it (and is created by it), but it is only by the present acts that any stabilization actually takes place. Immanent necessity is therefore not about what must happen, but about what must hold. Certain patterns, habits, relations, and constraints must remain stable for the configuration to continue. When they break, the configuration reorganizes and what was, is or will be necessary will change accordingly.

Let us borrow from Francisco Varela’s interpretation of Edmund Husserl’s analysis of temporality and take the present as the center, the past as the horizon actively shaping the present, and the future as the intended next moment toward which the present is already directed.[^1] What I consider necessary in this moment depends on what I intend the next moment to be, either tacitly or explicitly. The crucial point is that these dimensions are themselves dynamic. What I consider the present is already passing and is inherently future-oriented. This may be either a reason or a consequence of the general human difficulty of remaining in the present. The present is supposed to be all we have, yet it is never fully given: the present is the slipping of the future into the present and of the just-past into the past (cf Maurice Merleau-Ponty).

It seems, then, that prospective necessity collapses into immanent necessity, and immanent necessity collapses toward retrospective necessity. What is necessary in a given moment is what is required for the system to remain viable in the transition between what has been and what is about to come. What I take to be necessary in the present is only necessary as a link between retrospective and prospective necessity.

This approach does not deny that we have the power to change our present or even reshape our relation to the past. It only intends to highlight that both past and future constrain us in the present, and that this constraint is the very condition of freedom. Without constraint, there is no freedom. Where everything goes, nothing does.

[^1]: "There is always a center, the now moment with a focused intentional content (say, this room with my computer in front of me on which the letters I am typing are highlighted). This center is bounded by a horizon or fringe that is already past (I still hold the beginning of the sentence I just wrote), and it projects toward an intended next moment (this writing session is still unfinished). These horizons are mobile: this very moment which was present (and hence was not merely described, but lived as such) slips toward an immediately past present. Then it plunges further out of view: I do not hold it just as immediately, and I need an added depth to keep it at hand." (Varela, 1999)
