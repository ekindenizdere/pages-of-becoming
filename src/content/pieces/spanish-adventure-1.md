On the 12th of October, the party of two arrived at the fortified farmhouse in Altura, Spain, about 30 minutes from the big city of Valencia. The journey had started in Barcelona on the 11th of October, around 2 am. Having wasted some hours at Barcelona airport, mostly trying to turn the available resources (benches, jackets and softer clothes) into a bed, and searching for decent food and coffee (a search that, unbeknownst to the party, would continue in the days to come), the party of two set out to find a mode of transportation to Valencia. It was around 7 am, and the sky was neatly split in two: one side still as dark as night, the other carrying a mix of blue and pinkish early-morning colors. Birds were beginning to sing, rather sleepily, or this is how it felt to me, who hadn’t got any sleep on the self-made bench-bed, especially because other sleepy to-be passengers either saw the bench-bed and unsuccessfully tried to fashion one of their own, or opted to join the party in it. It was the first time I shared a bed with a complete stranger, and I was understandably unable to sleep and fully on guard. It was more important for the driver of the party to get some sleep, and, to my surprise, he managed to fit his rather large body in an S-shape, like a snake, between the armrests.

When the drive towards Valencia started, the sky had already gotten rid of its pitch-dark side and was now fully blue and clear, with palm trees looking like they belonged more to it than to the ground. Palm trees are very respectable trees. They are, in their unkempt state, a mixture of chaos at the bottom and order on top: Dionysus turning into Apollo. The dry leaves, turned greyish brown, or rather brownish grey, hang downwards like bushes, and the fresh leaves, fully erect, spike strongly into the sky.

The party of two drove towards their first stop before Valencia (or Altura, to be more precise): Calafell. They attempted to make some stops on the way, searching for a good view and good food. Only the former was attainable. The party was, at this point, unaware of the gastronomic horrors that awaited them in Calafell. They still entertained the naive notion of being presented with Spanish tomatoes that tasted like tomatoes, fresh veggies and bread infused with olive oil.

They stopped at the beach of Castelldefels, but before they could reach the sea, they got into a dispute with the parking meter. It refused their money at first, as if it had standards. When it finally accepted it, it declared that they did not have enough balance, while displaying, on its own screen and in perfectly legible numbers, more than enough. The party, sleepless and in no condition to argue with or rage against the machine, opted for insanity, as defined in the famous line persistently and wrongly attributed to Einstein: doing the same thing over and over again and expecting different results. They did the same thing over and over again. And to their surprise, insanity worked. This episode was the first of many disagreements with Spanish technology on this trip. Once the peace between man and machine was restored (through insanity), the party of two set off to make their first contact with the Spanish sea.

The big boy, the driver of the party, had made this stop unannounced, very proud of himself, knowing the glimpse of the sea and the salty smell induced by it was necessary to kickstart the Spanish adventure. Even though the beach was, in the words of the driver of the party, “government friendly” and made him feel like a “factory pig”, they were satisfied to see an uncountable number of people running, walking their dogs and doing yoga in groups of 5 to 10 on an early Sunday morning. This, now that I think about it, gave them hope: these healthy people must have some nice food. They were greatly misled. Again, unbeknownst to them, the gastronomic horrors were waiting.

Counting their first contact with the sea a success, they returned to their car, unaware that it would later become a “dancing car”, once GPS technology started struggling with the life below it as the party approached their farmhouse: on top of a mountain, as close as it gets to the middle of nowhere while being only half an hour away from the big city. But the car, as of this point, was very much a normal one as the party drove through beautiful, sun-bathed mountains, greenery on the one side and deep blue on the other, past Port Ginesta, with its large open spaces around the marina and its white architecture, giving a classical Mediterranean feeling but much larger in its construction than what one might find in Greece or south-western Turkey. Then through Garraf, where a Gaudían structure, Gaudí Garraf, caught my sleepy eye with its architecture, classically Spanish in its light brown color and chaotically in order like classic Gaudí, with some added cuteness from its miniature palm trees and cacti.

Biological needs that had already shown themselves in Castelldefels were unfortunately not cancelled out by gorgeous views, and the search for a toilet began. They ended up at a gas station, drinking terrible coffee, which, unfortunately, was not the worst they had experienced in Spain. It is a funny thing to go from the calmness of the beach in Castelldefels and the dreamy views of Garraf to an ugly, industrial gas station with what they audaciously call “coffee”.

After the gas station, they decided to drive to Calafell directly, without further stops. I had reserved a hotel room next to the beach, and even though the big boy was never fully happy with the factory-pig-feeling-inducing artificiality of the hotel, he said he could survive it for a day. I had hopes that the party could do better than surviving. In the end, they barely even survived. The big boy, having rested a bit in his snake form on the bench-bed for a couple of hours, had more cognitive capacity than I did. Sometimes one brain has to be shared by two.

The party reached the hotel and decided to grab a bite to eat before check-in. Calafell offered a beautiful natural landscape and ugly man-made structures. The population, maybe because it is off-season, consisted largely of older couples, many, many dogs that resembled their owners (as mister acute observer of the party pointed out) and some very unhealthy families. People, except for some that the party suspected of being very rich, were completely devoid of style, and the same was true of the restaurants: ugly signs, uncomfortably touristic places that had Korean fried chicken and Valencian paella on their menus, and people selling knock-off sneakers and African wooden sculptures by the side of the road.

The party managed to find a less than terrible-looking little restaurant that, at around 11 am, only had breakfast items on its menu. I drank two glasses of good, freshly pressed orange juice and a couple of glasses of white wine, and ate a very good jamón sandwich: the only good food I have eaten in Spain so far. The big boy also enjoyed some glasses of wine and had a French omelette with bread topped with tomato sauce. They got greedy and ordered two more sandwiches, one with tuna, the other with veggies and chicken. The vegetarian big boy had been thinking for a while that maybe, sometimes, he could eat some meat. This trip, which the party took on the occasion of his 30th birthday, could allow for this, he thought. He miscalculated. He got grossed out by the chicken and struggled a lot with the tuna sandwich. This was the end of his meat-eating fantasy. He decided not to do it again. I, for my part, enjoyed the chicken sandwich, but given the extremely oily mode of preparation, mixed with the physical struggles I had (having not slept, walking around like a zombie), I decided not to finish it and to pack both sandwiches, to maybe eat them at a later point (I could only eat close to half). The party set out to the hotel to get some well-deserved rest. They slept until 5:30 pm. They could have slept more, but they decided not to miss the sun.

They went back to the beach, a two-minute walk from their hotel. They could also see a bit of the sea from their weirdly shaped balcony, which held two plastic chairs whose legs stood half outside it, a perfect setup for an accidental death, were it not for the frosted glass panel that, once one sat down, blocked the sea view completely. I decided not to ponder the meaning of this balcony’s existence, but I can say now that if I ever feel useless, or even somewhat destructive and unnecessary, in the future, the image of this balcony will come to mind.

At the beach, the big boy activated his golden-retriever mode. He walked around, collected seashells, photographed people lying in the sun and reading books, and did push-ups with his feet on the low wall between the promenade and the beach. He teased me until I chased him a bit (very golden-retriever-like of me, too), and finally took me for a walk. They collected more seashells, the biggest the party had found on their trips together so far (his solo trips had produced far bigger ones), and reached a bridge where fresh water seemed to flow into the sea. There he found a black seashell that excited them both; when I unpacked yesterday, I found it had faded to a greyish color. Then he did pull-ups, jumping up to hold on to the metal beam of the bridge above him, while people walked by. A strange view for me to behold: under the bridge, an Austrian doing pull-ups, and above it, Spanish passers-by, mostly unhealthy, strolling past.

They then searched for a place to have a drink. I had a terrible piña colada and decided not to eat there. Praying to whatever god they could think of for some good food, they found a nice-looking restaurant: very expensive, but with very good food. They sat there and had some wild conversations about arrogance and confidence, and about my habits, which left me with some feelings of inferiority. Then they decided it was time to sleep again and returned to their hotel. Upon entering, they heard music on the right: old ladies with their husbands (or lovers, who am I to say?), dressed up in fancy clothes, wearing strong perfumes, big smiling lips with red lipstick, walking towards the music coming from the room with blue-purple-green lights, the disco lights I assume, a time capsule of the early 90s (again I assume; I was a kid then and have never been to a disco). The big boy suggested going in, predictably. But I do not dance, and for sure I do not dance in a 90s time capsule. And I needed to sleep. The party went up after struggling with technology once again, this time with the elevator; abandoning the fight prematurely, they took the stairs and reached their hotel room.

They woke up around 8 am on the 12th of October. They thought they were lucky because they had breakfast at the hotel. It had proven very difficult to hunt for food. If the hotel offered breakfast, it would make their lives easier, they thought. They probably also thought that, since it is a Mediterranean culture, how bad could hotel food be? It is difficult for me to write these words, to relive the experience, but I will be strong, continue with my third-person/first-person mixture of narrative and try to do justice to the experience, even though it is better experienced than read about. I would not wish this experience on my worst enemy. The party of two agreed that this was the worst gastronomic experience they had had in their almost 30 years on this earth: the earth that is rich with sun and sea and nutrition and taste. So buckle up, and find refuge in the fact that when you hit rock bottom, the only way forward is up.

The first shock they were confronted with was the long line at the breakfast room door, and a lady standing there checking breakfast passes. The party of two had lost theirs; they never thought it would or could be relevant, the white paper with some words on it that they did not bother to read. The doorkeeper lady placed them at a table next to the entrance, far away from the sea view, far away from the heart of the crowd. There were around a hundred people inside and two food and drink stations: one on the right side of the hall, and the second further away, in the far left corner. There were multiple lines in front of and around the stations, people clustering up at every chance they got. The big boy took the lead, managing to make space for himself in some sort of a line, grabbing two plates from somewhere and bravely entering the chaos. I tried to take care of drinks. My first attempt at getting what the label said was “coffee” produced a hot chocolate, or, to be more precise, a dark brown liquid of essentially sugar and some unknown substances. My second attempt delivered another brownish liquid, a lighter color than the unidentified sugary one, with the smell of dust and, upon inspection, a taste of Mediterranean mud fused with pine cones, watered down enough to allow the consumer to pretend they were drinking something akin to coffee, but much worse than the worst coffee imaginable.

After leaving these unidentified and unidentifiable cups of warm something at the table, I followed the big boy, imitating his bravery, towards the “food” station. At what I suspect was the beginning of the station stood a somewhat tall, serious-looking man, cracking eggs into a big pan full of oil. Amid the chaotic noise of the crowd, I could hear the cracks and the hissing sound of eggs hitting the oil. He must have thought that the amount of oil was not satisfactory, since he was, very calmly and in monotonous movements, using his big flat spoon to take the burning oil and pour it over the eggs, the eggs sizzling. He didn’t look up, but he also didn’t look at his oily creation; he was staring into the void, which he must have spent a long time locating, as, to my knowledge, there was not an inch of empty space to be found in this gate-kept breakfast room. Next to the egg-man, there were different versions of tomato sauce; further along the way, some slices that looked like tomatoes, some larger than a full-sized tomato and most of them a pale pink color. Further down, some bacon, some other type of meat, no jamón, and then some sweets and other things I could not get a glimpse of, as the bigger portion of the human cluster was blocking my view. At the bread section, there was a toaster that warmed up the surroundings significantly and moved so slowly, with rhythmic machine sounds, as to remind one of some sort of auto-part factory in a low-budget sci-fi movie. One attempt to toast was not enough; one needed a second and a third try. My patience was so completely nullified halfway through the toasting process that I ended it prematurely, taking the bread the machine threw onto the plate below it and not furthering my attempts. This marks the third episode of struggle with technology.

After all this, the party of two still could not begin to imagine the horrid taste. Bad taste, for sure; that was guaranteed. But this non-taste, mixed with heavy oil and dusty, metallic fragments, was beyond the imaginative capacities the party possessed. They looked around to get a sense of how others dealt with this difficult situation and, to their surprise, found masses of people enjoying what they pretended was food. A big portion of people opted for more sugar, with plates full of Nutella and glasses full of juice that, the party of two decided, tasted like sugar mixed with more sugar and topped with sugary fruit syrup. They observed people eating the tomato sauce, which, the party of two decided, tasted like motor oil. One person’s metal is another person’s nutrition, it seems. As the party of two had decided to leave the food uneaten, they waited for the gatekeeper to leave her post so as not to receive any questions about the quality of the food. They made a run for it, more metaphorically speaking than literally.
