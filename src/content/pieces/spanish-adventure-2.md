Having escaped from the gatekeeper of the breakfast room, the party of two returned their room keys and left for the car with their bags. I do not remember when the big boy made his plan on where to go next; I was not involved in plans. When the party was in Vienna, it was decided that planning was to fall under the big boy’s jurisdiction. This approach had proved successful when the party was in Greece. Once again on Mediterranean soil, my job was simply to be, a task I felt confident I could handle and, in hindsight, carried out with flying colors. I was blissfully unaware that my lack of planning would become a problem when things escalated in Miravet, where a waiter carried off the table from under the big boy’s elbows. This we will come back to.

They got into their little car, stored their bags in a less than orderly fashion on the back seat, made themselves comfortable, buckled up and started driving. The first stop the big boy had decided on was El Vendrell. The weather was warm, around 30 degrees, and the roads offered enough empty space for a calm trip, the ideal circumstances for the party to discuss the breakfast disaster until they reached El Vendrell. There, the choice was presented to me: either go to the sea directly or go for coffee. I opted for the latter, surprising no one.

The party laid eyes on a luxurious-looking place, and after trying to understand whether it was a restaurant, a hotel or a hotel’s restaurant, they decided to sit at one of the tables outside, looking onto a view of some families playing billiards. They first waited for the waiter or waitress to come, in vain. Then, after accepting their fate, they started the discussion of which member of the party should be tasked with going in and ordering. I used my lack of Spanish as an excuse, which did not work, as the task required no language skills whatsoever. Then I simply stood my ground, and the big boy, however irritated he was with the situation, had to go in. He is a doer. He “does” regardless of whether he is tired, annoyed, or whether his partner has been getting on his nerves most of the day. He rarely loses his smile (but when he does, it is a scary sight to behold, which we will come back to in the upcoming chapters), and he “does” what he does. So he “did”. He went in. I was alone with the sun for a while, sharing a brief moment of romance until he came back out. When he is around, the sun is his; they find their way to each other somehow. He came out without eradicating the uncertainty of the situation: was the party to expect someone to come with coffee, someone to take their order, or no one at all? A couple of minutes later, to their surprise, a young woman came with two cups of coffee, which were not terrible and which cost around 5 euros, very cheap by Viennese standards.

After coffee, the party was to walk towards the sea, or to be more precise, to the thermal waters of El Riuet de Coma-ruga, the second place where the party saw a river seamlessly merging with the sea. Before the Spanish adventure, I had experienced this only once, in Turkey. It is not a banal sight, and for some reason it meant something to me to stand at a point where waters meet. From the ground, through the short-sighted goggles that every human being wears from birth and can never take off, the sight is of two separate bodies uniting. But I stood there, at the meeting point, for the second time in Spain, and I did not see any indication that the river was a river and not the sea, or that the sea was the sea and not the river. There is, of course, the difference in the mineral composition of the water, and of course the temperature, but where they meet, that point is seamless: they do not mesh into each other, but they are not completely separate either; they are not one, not two. Anyway, before a big chunk of my readers, and more importantly my big boy, gets bored with this chain of abstract non-thought, I will get back to our story.

Now, at this point, I was given a task: to locate the little river on Google Maps, so that the planner of the party would know whether walking was reasonable or the car should be put to further use. I have no excuse for having failed this task. I might blame it on tiredness and the cognitive impairment that comes with it, but then again, there are excuses and there are results. Well, in this case there is neither: there is only a lack of results. I could not find what was right in front of my eyes. I managed to locate the sea, but the little river was hiding from me. The big boy was terribly confused at this point. “So you are telling me,” he cried out after locating it himself, “you did not see this big blue thing here?” (pointing at my phone screen). The question was repeated a couple of times in different forms: “So you are telling me there is no river here?”, “So you really did not see the blue thing here?”, “Here!” and so on. So, in the end, regardless of the process itself, the river was located and the party started marching on.

The big boy was excited. In his planning session, he had seen pictures of a wall of grey stones with an arch at its bottom and, on its upper half, orange tiles neatly placed inside blue ones. In blue capital letters, they read “El Riuet de Coma-ruga” (the little river of Coma-ruga) and below, also in capitals, “Sortida al mar de les aigües de l’estany termal” (outlet to the sea of the waters of the thermal lake). In those photos, which he showed me after I had seen the real thing, the mineral water came out of the arch at the bottom of the wall. This was the thing that excited the big boy the most. The water then gathered right in front of the wall, creating a small pool where people sat, healing (or so the big boy assumed). From the pool, the healing water ran to the sea, surrounded and guided by low walls on both sides and crossed by small footbridges. This was the picture he was hunting for, and the picture that would cause his initial disappointment. What he saw was that even though the beautiful blue and orange tiles still proudly announced the little river’s path, the river no longer came out of the arch below the wall, and so the pool was gone. The walls around the water were gone, as were the footbridges. With nothing left to hold it in line, the water spread out, in no hurry to reach anything. The big boy gave his initial rating: 6 out of 10.

The little river was thermal, mineral water. Now, let me open a parenthesis here to be careful with the word *thermal*. It is true that, both originally and today, it means hot water. It comes from the Greek *thermē*, heat, and was borrowed by the Romans for their hot baths and then by modern English for hot springs. The word has been promising warmth for a couple of thousand years. El Riuet keeps a steady 18 to 21 degrees all year, but whether this counts as warm or not is left for the sea to decide. If you go there in summer, you will be misled, because you will find the little river around 10 degrees colder than the Mediterranean. If you go there in winter, *thermal* is a perfectly justified description. The party arrived in October, and according to my very unreliable measuring instruments, a couple of toes dipped in the sea, the two waters were the same temperature, around 20 degrees. Given the circumstances, the party must conclude that they did not have enough data to judge whether *thermal* is the right word. It pleases me, however, that sometimes the sea is the measure of all things.

The big boy swam in the river, and afterwards also in the sea. As usual, he collected seashells and presented them for my approval. Some of them ended up in the hands of a little boy who sat next to him on the plane back to Vienna, apparently as a prize for having annoyed the big boy with very intelligent and pressing questions, amongst which I can, with astonishment, count the following two: “What are you reading?” and “Why are you reading it?”. When I earn my seashells, it is not because of my ability to annoy the big boy (that ability is evergreen), but because there are seashells to be hunted. These shells, like the ones in Calafell, were rather large and more often than not light brown mixed with some purple. The party could only have dreamt of them in Greece, when the big boy went on his seashell hunt and returned with small and smaller ones, which I afterwards glued onto a piece of rock (or is it a bone?) and left to dry under the sun in Bregenz. This was not my most successful arts project, but not the least successful one either.

As the big boy was swimming in the healing waters, I followed him as far as I could, on foot. I called it when the water reached my belly, stopped moving and opted for standing, which was in line with my main task, “to be”. When he swam towards me and I put a foot on his back, trying to sink him, he was not amused. No, sir. He tried his best to annoy me back, splashing cold water on me, which resulted in me almost knocking over some kids in my attempt to escape the water. The party also witnessed an interesting sight: a rather big, old black lady, standing, being “healed” by a young white girl kneeling in front of her in the water, who took sand from the bottom and rubbed it onto the old lady’s legs.

The events that followed, namely the party sitting in the sun reading after the big boy’s dip in the sea, and then finding a workout station on their way out, where he did his pull-ups and what have you, caused his rating to jump to a generous 8 out of 10. After the workout, the party walked towards the car. The big boy used my scarf, the one I have had since high school, the characteristic piece of clothing I was known for, which I “stole” from my mom after it was given to her as a gift by my dad’s best friend’s Italian wife. He used it to change out of his swimming trunks, and the party jumped into the car and drove to their next destination: Miravet.

In the car, I noticed that I had received more than 60 text messages from the Airbnb host, one of which contained crucial information. Reading messages is sometimes very difficult: when one is immersed in the immediate reality, the distant reality presented by the smartphone is not easy to take in, to engage with. So the party left the messages unread and drove towards Miravet, hoping to grab a bite there. The gastronomic horrors of the previous chapter, again unbeknownst to the party, were to continue.

Miravet was a natural delight and a social disaster. I have not seen many places as beautiful as the Castillo de Miravet: a castle built on top of a hill, with the town spreading unevenly beneath it down to the bank of a greenish river surrounded by trees, reminiscent of Amalfi. Towards the right-hand side of the town, seen from afar, there is a wall, and low in it, at the waterline, an opening through a stone platform that seems to carry the terrace of one of the houses.

The party spent some time taking in the heavenly view, then moved towards the docks and sat there with their feet in the water, letting the fish come around, first curiously and then ambitiously, biting off what they could of the human flesh of the feet. The big boy let them. I did not. I cautiously played with them, pulling my feet away when they came closer. As the party of two moved up, the big boy tried to climb the riverside wall, to no one’s surprise. Sitting on the wall, I saw some people with a dog in the middle of them, a delightful picture.

The party was starving, but they thought this was not a problem, as they saw some restaurants and a great deal of activity up the wall. There were many youngsters, most of them wearing the same red shirt (or was it green?); some were running around throwing water at each other, some running shirtless, with a lot of screams and laughter. Something very fun was going on that the party was neither part of nor could grasp. They also had little motivation to understand, as they were hunting for food. There were two restaurants; they opted for the one further away from the crowd. They walked around awkwardly for a while, seeing some empty tables full of dirty plates indicating that someone had eaten there. The big boy asked the waitress, and the answer was “food, no chance; drinks, maybe”. They could not make sense of the answer, so they continued to walk around awkwardly. I walked further away from him and left him to figure out the mess, which was neither the best idea nor the kindest action to take. But I was confused, slightly embarrassed and significantly annoyed by the lack of recognition the party received, and the restaurant’s refusal to serve was triggering some not very kind emotions in me. The walking around continued, the party met in the middle again, and he decided to try again, only to get the same answer. Then he, as the planning officer, searched for another restaurant up the hill. The path was gorgeous: the medieval air of light brown surroundings, largely free of crowds, narrow passages between brick-covered walls, a smell of cold air and humidity that was pleasant and now resists my attempts to put it into words. The party walked, reached the other restaurant, and the big boy suggested a radical approach: they were to sit down without asking and see what happened. He sat down at a table; I did not. I was watching. The waiter came after a few minutes, and I heard them exchange some words, my confusion lingering. I was told that the waiter “will see”. The big boy sat there, calm, the sun on his face (as usual, his face always finds a way to meet the sun). A couple of minutes later, after I had taken a short stroll to explore the surroundings, I saw the waiter coming over while the big boy was still seated, reaching in, grabbing the table and taking it away. The big boy sat there, his table taken away but his peace undisturbed, with an air of nakedness, of table-less-ness, confused I assume, but not showing it. He kept sitting. I kept being away. When he gave up, he, I must add rightfully, got upset with me: “If you do not like the plan I make, feel free to suggest one yourself.” They walked towards the car and started driving, hoping to find something on the way.

They stopped at a marina, the location of which I am unable to recover, and the first restaurant they went to refused to serve them: either it was not open or it was closing. The second one seemed very much open, with some people inside who seemed to enjoy their food and some outside, wearing cycling gear, enjoying their drinks. The menu included an extensive section on pizza and, other than that, only salads and desserts. The party assumed that this restaurant was some sort of pizzeria and ordered a margherita and a tomato salad. First came the appetizer: olives. I was excited. Olives are the palm trees of gastronomy. They are very respectable. However, they arrived with a strong smell. This did not stop me from trying. As I reached in to take an olive, I discovered something interesting: a piece of white mass that looked like and smelled like a clove of garlic. And it indeed was a clove of garlic! I have, in my almost 30 years, never seen olives marinated and served with garlic. I respect olives. So I tried one nevertheless. What I tasted was, well, garlic fused with some mushy structure that looked like an olive. But it was okay, this was just the appetizer. Then came the tomato salad. I counted the tomatoes, still smelling the garlic olives, and there were 6 slices. None of them tasted like a tomato. They were spread on top of some lettuce and barely sliced onions, with some cucumber as the cherry on top. But it was okay, this was only the salad. Then came the pizza. I noticed something: it looked like the frozen pizza you buy in a grocery store, it smelled like frozen pizza, and soon I discovered it tasted like frozen pizza. It was frozen pizza. They paid and left.

They decided that maybe it was best to cook for themselves and not outsource the basic needs of humanity. They started searching for a grocery store and tried a few that were supposed to be open according to Google Maps but looked very much closed upon closer inspection. After the second failed grocery attempt, I decided to read the Airbnb lady’s 60+ messages. She was informing the party that it was a public holiday, Día de la Hispanidad. This explained the closed shops, the empty kitchens and perhaps the youngsters in red shirts. It did not explain the frozen pizza.

The Airbnb lady suggested the gas station for food. The party took her advice, set their GPS, which was working reasonably well at this point, to the gas station and drove. It was getting late and dark. At the gas station, they bought coffee, oat milk, pasta and pasta sauce. This was to turn into one of the best meals they had in Spain. With their groceries, they drove further towards the Airbnb, the fortified farmhouse that they called “the castle”.

It was pitch-dark. At some point they left the conventional road and turned onto one made mainly of small stones crackling under the wheels. They could only see 2 meters ahead, maybe less, in the headlights; all around, darkness embraced them in the middle of nowhere, and they heard only the small rocks jumping around and hitting the car’s belly. They drove below 40 km/h. Slowly but surely, they drove. Then, all of a sudden, they saw it: a tall, square block, and two slim round towers of a pinkish color standing on either side of a dark wooden double door with a rounded arch. They had reached Masia de San Juan. Their castle.
